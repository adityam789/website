## No losers

On 16 November I gave a speech at a Gita Jayanti competition. I didn't win. I'm fine with that, mostly because the room was full of people who had spent weeks with the same 700 verses and found different things in them. Nobody left with nothing. I left with a list of things I want to read.

This is the speech, rewritten to be read. I usually write about systems here. This is the other half of the notebook.

## Arjuna freezes

The Gita opens at the worst possible moment. Two armies face each other, and Arjuna, the best archer alive, looks across and sees his teachers, grandfathers, cousins and friends. His hands shake, his mouth dries up, and the bow slips out of his hand (1.28-30). Then he says the most honest thing in the book: I don't know what is right anymore. Teach me (2.7).

What I like is *why* he freezes. It isn't cowardice. It's compassion. He is asking what a victory is worth if it's built on the people he loves. Krishna doesn't answer with a pep talk. He gives a whole philosophy of how to act, and the next 17 chapters are that answer.

You don't need a battlefield to recognise this. A job that pays well and feels empty. Burnout that makes you question the whole career. A gap between who you are and who you're pretending to be. Same freeze, smaller bow.

## Three paths, one destination

The Gita doesn't offer one formula. It offers three, and a traditional reading groups the chapters accordingly:

| Path | Idea | Chapters |
|---|---|---|
| Karma Yoga | Act well, don't cling to the result | 1-6 |
| Bhakti Yoga | Serve something larger, with love | 7-12 |
| Jnana Yoga | Understand what's beneath the surface | 13-18 |

They overlap more than the table suggests. I spoke mostly about Karma Yoga, because early in a career you're in the arena every day, and the other two kept showing up anyway.

I organised the talk around the classical four stages of life, since the Gita's advice changes with where you stand.

## Stage 1: the student (Brahmacharya)

The Katha Upanishad gives an image the Gita builds on. The self is the passenger in a chariot. The body is the chariot, the intellect is the charioteer, the mind is the reins and the senses are the horses. If the horses are wild and the charioteer is asleep, the chariot leaves the road.

The warning that goes with it is that knowing isn't enough. Dhritarashtra, the blind king, heard the entire Gita narrated to him in real time. He knew what was right and chose what was comfortable, because he couldn't let go of his sons. The student stage is about learning to *apply*, and that comes down to three things.

**Know yourself.** Not what your parents want, what pays, or what looks good on LinkedIn. The Gita calls your nature your *svadharma*, and 3.35 says it's better to do your own duty imperfectly than someone else's well. A fish forced to climb a tree spends its life thinking it's stupid. A lot of us are fish.

**Name your duty.** Yajnavalkya tells King Janaka that you become what your deep, driving desire is. Duty isn't arbitrary: it comes from your nature, your stage of life and what the world needs from you.

**Act, without ego.** Action is better than inaction (3.8). You can't even maintain your body without it. So the question was never *whether* to act. It's whether you act attached or free.

## Stage 2: the householder (Grihastha)

This is where most of us are. Rent is due, people depend on us, the manager is watching. We want the promotion and the security, and somewhere along the way we start checking Slack at midnight and comparing ourselves to everyone.

Krishna's first move is to deal with fear itself. The self is like the wearer of clothes, and the body, the job title and the bank balance are the clothes (2.22). Things come and go, and what's watching them doesn't.

Then comes the most quoted verse in the book, 2.47: you have a right to the work, never to its fruits. When I first heard it I thought: nice, but I have bills. Here is how I'd put it now. Imagine a presentation tomorrow that your promotion hangs on. If tonight goes on *will they like it, what if I mess up, what if she does better*, your mind fractures and you're worse at the thing. If tonight goes on *is this clear, is this useful, is this my best work*, you get into flow. The paradox is that letting go of the outcome tends to improve it.

The honest follow-up is that our minds don't cooperate. Arjuna says so himself: the mind is restless and stubborn, harder to control than wind (6.34). Krishna doesn't say "think positive". He says practice and detachment, and he gets practical. Moderation in food, sleep and work (6.16-17). A few minutes of meditation. Gradual, not overnight. The mind can be your friend or your enemy (6.5), and training it is what makes the rest possible.

Then the shift that surprised me. When the work is steady and the mind is steady, it begins to feel like offering rather than achieving. 9.27: whatever you do, make it an offering. Burnout fades when work is aimed at something beyond the paycheck.

## Stages 3 and 4: reflection and renunciation

Ten or twenty years in, priorities move. You care less about the promotion and more about what you leave and who you mentor. You've seen enough booms, busts and rises and falls to know nothing is permanent.

The Gita's answer to impermanence is equanimity: treat pleasure and pain, gain and loss, victory and defeat alike (2.38). It doesn't say ignore them. It says see that both are temporary and both are teachers.

Two characters stood out to me here. Sanjaya, who narrates the war, sees it with perfect clarity while everyone else is caught in fear and attachment. Not clouded by ambition, not distorted by what he wants. That's the gift of the later stages: seeing what is actually there. And the Bhagavata Purana tells of King Ambarisha, a householder with a kingdom to run, whose plain devotion humbled sages. You don't have to leave the world to find what you're looking for. You can find it in the work.

One more image, from the Svetasvatara Upanishad: a wheel of cosmic consciousness that turns forever, with all creatures bound to it as long as they believe they're separate from it. We aren't trying to stop the wheel of success and failure. We're noticing we're not on it.

## Back to the battlefield

My favourite detail is the ending. After 700 verses, Krishna's last word to Arjuna isn't an order (18.63). He says he has shared everything, to reflect on it fully and then do as he wishes. He gives the choice back. And Arjuna says the confusion is gone and he will act.

That's the promise: not escape, not a detachment that makes you passive, but clarity that lets you act. One more thought I added near the end: maybe the Mahabharata isn't only history. Maybe Kurukshetra is inside each of us, with ego against humility, greed against contentment and fear against courage, and we decide who wins every day through small choices.

## The curriculum for Monday morning

1. **Know yourself.** Stop chasing someone else's dharma. Find yours.
2. **Act without clinging.** Do excellent work, then release the outcome. Watch your process, not your anxiety.
3. **Master your mind.** Five minutes of meditation counts. Just start.
4. **Serve with purpose.** Remember why the work matters beyond the paycheck.

And through it all, practise equanimity. Success and failure are both temporary.

The Gita isn't asking anyone to become a monk. It's asking for wise professionals: people who excel without being imprisoned by success, who care without being controlled by outcomes, and who serve without needing constant validation.

Our education begins now.
